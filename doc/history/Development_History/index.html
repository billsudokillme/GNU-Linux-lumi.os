Development_History

Version 0.1

Although the currently released system began its development in early July 2026, the Lumi OS project actually started in late March 2025. Depending on how you look at the project, there’s a funny—or ironic—story behind it. Back before 2025, specifically between late 2024 and January 2025 (though I can’t pinpoint the exact timing), I watched a video titled "What is Linux?" The video discussed the history of Linux, GNU, free software, UNIX, and simplified how operating systems work. I didn’t pay much attention at first, but the explanation, the unfamiliar information, and the narrator's storytelling style stuck with me for months. I started following his channel and a few other YouTubers who talked about Linux, privacy, and free software in general. However, what truly helped me learn about Linux and free software correctly was that Al-Waqqad—unlike the vast majority of YouTubers covering Linux—had actual hands-on experience and genuine opinions on every topic. There was no bias, recycled chatter, or superficial takes backed by a week's worth of experience without proper study. This contrast was especially clear compared to most Arabic content creators in the field. Thanks to him, building a deep, authentic understanding of Linux took me weeks and months instead of the years I spent when trying to learn programming and software mechanics from inaccurate or biased sources. That is why I wrote this section and included links to his accounts and videos below: as a tribute to him for spreading knowledge properly, even if in a language not spoken by the majority. Out of those ideas and that deeper understanding, a thought crossed my mind: "If Linux, GNU tools, and open-source software make it so easy to build something great if you put in the work, why don't I build my own distribution?" That idea persisted for a long time, and its goals evolved as I waited to get a computer capable of making it happen. But there was no hope of getting a PC or a laptop—even one with low specs—and that situation dragged on for months. Then, I discovered that Al-Waqqad had explained how to install and customize Linux Mint. I remembered an old laptop we had that took half an hour just to boot Windows and minutes to open Google, with a screen full of black spots. Using a USB drive we owned, I installed Linux Mint on it. It ran a bit lighter, though still somewhat sluggish, and I didn't even understand how to use the terminal back then. During those first few days, I relied on ChatGPT to figure out the system, and a few days later, I decided to start my own OS project—which I later named Lumi. Due to the laptop's limited hardware, my own lack of experience, and the machine entering fallback mode every few hours, compiling the kernel on default settings took hours. Nevertheless, I kept working on the project for 5 to 8 hours a day, experimenting with building on various distributions—Debian, Ubuntu, Alpine, and many others—as well as using BusyBox and building the system from scratch. I tried almost every available method you could think of, except Buildroot, which I hadn't heard of at the time. I worked for hours, days, and weeks, only to shut the laptop down after a failure, come back a few days later, and try again—not to mention dealing with our dear friend, migraine attacks. This cycle continued for months until one day, I lost my temper and pressed down on the screen. The display grew even darker, and a few days later, the laptop stopped working altogether due to an electrical failure. Throughout most of that period, I had come very close to my goal multiple times, but I kept failing because I didn't know how to properly customize grub.cfg. Later, in late August, we bought a new laptop with good specifications for my brother's studies. Since it belonged to him, I installed WSL to continue working on the OS and gave Buildroot a try. Although it didn't do exactly what I wanted, it gave me a strong morale boost because the system actually booted inside a virtual environment. All along, I was slowly learning new principles of system development until July 12 arrived, and I created a full, stable system: Version 0.1. Well, at least I didn't waste my time.
